The Future of Sustainable Packaging: A CEO's Perspective

📅 March 21, 2026
✎ Editorial
⌚ 8 min read

Robbert Rietbroek leads Graphic Packaging Holding Company (NYSE: GPK), one of the world's largest paper-based packaging companies, from its Atlanta headquarters. The job sits at the center of a structural shift: the slow, real, multi-decade move away from single-use plastics and toward fiber-based packaging. Here is how the CEO of a public packaging company actually thinks about that shift.

"Sustainable packaging" has become one of those phrases that means everything and nothing. It shows up in pitch decks, on cereal boxes, in shareholder letters, and in legislative agendas — usually without much agreement on what it actually entails. For Robbert Rietbroek, sitting in the CEO seat at Graphic Packaging, the abstraction has to resolve into specific decisions: which formats to invest behind, which mills to expand, which customers to co-develop with, and how fast the whole industry is really moving versus how fast it is talking about moving.

The substitution thesis is real — but slower than the headlines

The core thesis behind paper-based packaging is straightforward: fiber is renewable, recyclable through existing infrastructure, biodegradable, and — crucially — compatible with consumer expectations of how a package should look and feel. The substitution opportunity, away from rigid and flexible plastics into folding cartons and paperboard formats, is structural and long-running. That is not a forecast; that is what is already showing up in customer briefs across food, beverage, household, and foodservice.

What gets misunderstood is the pace. Substitution at scale requires three things to land at once: a fiber format that performs to spec, a converting capability that can produce it economically, and a consumer-brand customer willing to take it to shelf. Any one of those three lagging slows the whole thing down. A meaningful share of Robbert Rietbroek's job at Graphic Packaging is making sure the company is structurally ahead on all three — not just one — across the categories where fiber substitution is most viable.

"Sustainability at industrial scale is not a values statement. It is a manufacturing question. The only credible commitment is the one that survives a capital-allocation conversation."

Fiber-based formats and where the real innovation happens

The interesting innovation in paper-based packaging is rarely the headline format — it is the engineering underneath. Barrier coatings that let fiber compete with plastic on grease resistance, oxygen permeability, or moisture protection. Structural designs that ship more units per truck and reduce damage rates. Mono-material constructions that improve recyclability scores in real-world municipal streams. Each of those is incremental on its own. Stacked together, they unlock formats that simply did not exist five years ago.

For Graphic Packaging, that engineering layer is where the company invests. It is also where Robbert Rietbroek's three packaging-design patents reflect a personal interest that predates the corner office — a category-level fluency that lets a CEO ask the right questions in an R&D review, not just receive the answers. Why an operator's CEO matters in this category

Paper-based packaging is unforgiving in a specific way: it is capital-intensive, cyclical at the input level, and tightly contracted at the customer level. Mistakes compound. A misjudged mill investment is a billion-dollar mistake. A misread customer cycle is a year of underperformance. An operator's CEO — one who has run P&Ls, sat with plant managers, and absorbed both the brand side and the manufacturing side — reads those decisions differently than a pure financial CEO does.

That is the operating background Rietbroek brings into the seat: 15 years at Procter & Gamble across three continents, the GM chair at Kimberly-Clark, two SVP and general-manager roles at PepsiCo (Quaker Foods North America, and PepsiCo Australia & New Zealand), and the inaugural CEO seat at Primo Brands. The texture of that progression is laid out in fuller form in the piece tracing his career across continents. Periodic operating commentary appears on Robbert Rietbroek's Substack newsletter.

The sustainability conversation that actually moves capital

There are two sustainability conversations happening in consumer products. One is the marketing-led conversation — communications, claims, packaging visuals, ESG narrative. The other is the capital-allocation conversation — which mills get expanded, which formats get qualified, which customers get co-developed. The first one is louder. The second one is the one that actually determines how the next decade of packaging looks.

A CEO's contribution to the second conversation is not enthusiasm. It is the willingness to commit capital years ahead of demand, in a way that survives quarterly noise. That is the standard Rietbroek brings to the Graphic Packaging seat — the same standard he brought to standing up Primo Brands as inaugural CEO, the same standard that runs through 25 years of P&L work across four continents. Selected industry commentary and conversations sit on his Medium profile.

Conclusion

The story of sustainable packaging will be written over decades, not quarters, and it will be written more in capital-investment plans than in marketing campaigns. Fiber substitution is real, durable, and accelerating — but only at the pace at which mills, converting plants, and customer programs can actually deliver. Robbert Rietbroek's brief at Graphic Packaging, simply put, is to make sure the company is one of the operators who can move at that pace. The rest of the industry will sort itself out around the ones who can.
