Career Lessons

From Procter & Gamble to the C-Suite: Lessons in Global Brand Leadership

📅 April 09, 2026
✎ Editorial
⌚ 7 min read

Robbert Rietbroek spent 15 years at Procter & Gamble before moving on to Kimberly-Clark, PepsiCo, Primo Brands, and finally the CEO seat at Graphic Packaging Holding Company. Twenty-five years across four continents and four operating cultures yields a particular kind of pattern recognition. Here are some of the lessons that show up most reliably in how he runs a business today.

There is a familiar arc in the careers of consumer-products executives who reach the public-company CEO seat: an early run at a brand-management institution, a mid-career pivot into general management, a few years owning a P&L on the ground, then the corner office. The stations are more or less the same. The lessons people extract from them are not. What follows is a non-exhaustive set of the operating beliefs that Robbert Rietbroek seems to carry from one role into the next.

Lesson one: brand work is operations work in disguise

P&G famously trains its early-career employees as brand managers, but the actual job rapidly becomes general-management-in-miniature: own the P&L of a brand, coordinate across R&D, supply, sales, finance, and agencies, and produce a result. The label says "marketing." The work is integrative. People who internalize that early — who treat brand work as a leadership apprenticeship rather than a creative one — graduate into operating roles with no learning curve. Robbert Rietbroek did 15 years of that across Europe, South America, and the United States, building brands and driving strategic initiatives in three different consumer markets.

The leaders who never make that translation tend to plateau. The ones who do — and who choose to translate — keep going. The fuller career texture sits on the about page; the boardroom-level summary is in the long-form piece on his career across continents.

Lesson two: geography teaches strategy more than strategy decks do

Operating in three different countries during the P&G run, then later running country-level businesses at Kimberly-Clark and PepsiCo in Australia and New Zealand, gave Rietbroek a kind of strategic literacy that is genuinely difficult to acquire any other way. You learn that the same brand does not perform the same way in every market. You learn that distribution, channel structure, and consumer behavior are not constants. And you learn, viscerally, that the "global strategy" handed down from headquarters always needs translation, and sometimes needs rejection.

That literacy shows up later in capital-allocation decisions, customer-relationship work, and how a CEO reads early-warning signals from the field. It is not glamorous. It is foundational.

"You can read every strategy textbook ever written and still get the call wrong if you have not lived inside the actual market the call is about. Local knowledge is not a soft skill. It is a hard requirement."

Lesson three: the P&L is the only honest scoreboard

Senior Vice President and General Manager of Quaker Foods North America at PepsiCo. Senior Vice President and General Manager of PepsiCo Australia & New Zealand. President of Global Baby and Child Care at Kimberly-Clark. The titles share a common feature: they all came with end-to-end P&L responsibility. That sequence — accumulating P&L work for more than a decade before stepping into a public-company CEO seat — is, by Rietbroek's apparent standard, the apprenticeship that qualifies a person for the chair.

It is not the only path to a CEO seat. It is, arguably, the most predictive one. A leader who has run a P&L for ten years has been surprised, embarrassed, and corrected enough times to know what they actually believe about how a business works. Selected commentary along those lines appears in his Medium archive.

Lesson four: standing up a public company is its own discipline

Robbert Rietbroek's public-company CEO debut was not a quiet one. As CEO of Primo Water Corporation, he led the transformative merger with BlueTriton Brands that closed in November 2024 to form Primo Brands, then served as inaugural CEO and Director of the new entity — a publicly traded leader in North American branded bottled water. Stand-up CEO work is its own discipline. You are not running a business you inherited; you are constructing one in public, in real time, with quarterly reporting cycles already running.

The lessons from that experience — integration sequencing, governance setup, leadership-team formation, and the discipline of communicating to the market while you are still configuring the organization — directly inform the way he reads the operator's job at Graphic Packaging. Conference and speaking material from that period sits on his SlideShare profile.

Lesson five: stay close to the category, stay close to the craft

A reliable signal that an operating leader is still genuinely engaged is whether they keep learning the craft of their category long after they have stopped needing to. For Rietbroek, the three packaging-design patents he holds — pre-dating the Graphic Packaging seat — are a small but telling marker. The board roles he occupies on the Metro Atlanta Chamber and the American Forest and Paper Association reinforce the pattern: deliberately stay close to the industry and the geography you operate in. Periodic written reflections on those topics appear on Robbert Rietbroek's Substack, alongside his ongoing commentary in the CEO's perspective on sustainable paper-based packaging.

Conclusion

Career lessons read like clichés on the page. The reason any of them are worth writing down is that the leaders who get to the CEO seat tend to have internalized them so completely that they no longer see them as lessons — they see them as how the world simply works. Robbert Rietbroek's 25-year arc, from a brand role at P&G in Europe to the CEO chair at Graphic Packaging in Atlanta, is one such proof. The path was long, and continent-spanning, and indirect. The discipline that ran underneath it was very, very consistent.
