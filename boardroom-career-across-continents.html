Inside the Boardroom: How Robbert Rietbroek Built a Career Across Continents

📅 February 14, 2026
✎ Editorial
⌚ 7 min read

Robbert Rietbroek's career reads like a tour of the modern consumer-products industry: 15 years at Procter & Gamble across three continents, leadership stints at Kimberly-Clark and PepsiCo on opposite sides of the world, the inaugural CEO seat at Primo Brands, and now the corner office at Graphic Packaging Holding Company in Atlanta. The geography is the headline. The discipline underneath it is the story.

Most CEO biographies look tidy in retrospect, organized into clean ascending arrows on a LinkedIn page. The real arc is rarely that linear. For Rietbroek — now President and CEO of Graphic Packaging (NYSE: GPK) — the path took him from Maastricht to Cincinnati to São Paulo to Sydney to Chicago to Tampa to Atlanta. What kept it coherent was a single operating thesis: take a brand or business unit, sharpen its strategy, raise its operating standard, and grow it. The geography changed. The job description didn't.

The P&G foundation

The first 15 years of Robbert Rietbroek's career were spent at Procter & Gamble, in a sequence of roles across Europe, South America, and the United States. P&G is widely understood as the institution that effectively invented modern brand management; what it gives the people who survive its early-career gauntlet is something more specific — a way of breaking down a market into shoppers, channels, and category structure, and a discipline of writing strategy down before executing it.

By the time he left, Rietbroek had built brands inside three very different consumer markets and worked under three regional cultures. That mattered later. A leader who has only operated in one country tends to assume the local consumer is the universal consumer; a leader who has done strategic initiatives and brand development in Madrid, in Caracas, and in Cincinnati does not make that mistake. The texture of that period is sketched in fuller form on the about page.

"You do not learn how to run a global business by reading about global business. You learn it by being the person on the ground when the local market does not behave the way the headquarters slide deck said it would."

Kimberly-Clark and the GM chair

After P&G, Rietbroek moved to Kimberly-Clark, where he eventually led the company's Global Baby and Child Care business — a P&L spanning multiple continents, multiple categories, and the kind of operational complexity that resists neat slide decks. Before the global role, he ran Kimberly-Clark Australia & New Zealand, which is where the move from brand work into general management really happened.

Running a country business is the moment a marketer becomes an operator. You stop owning the message and start owning the result. You inherit the supply chain, the trade investment, the people, and the quarterly number. For Robbert Rietbroek, that transition meant absorbing a different kind of literacy — reading a P&L, sitting with a plant manager, negotiating with a major customer. The combination of brand instinct and operating literacy is what later qualified him to run public companies.

PepsiCo, Primo, and the public-company seat

PepsiCo gave Rietbroek two of his most demanding general-management roles: Senior Vice President and General Manager of Quaker Foods North America, and earlier the same title in Australia and New Zealand. Quaker is one of the most iconic American breakfast portfolios; running it meant directing brand management, operational excellence, and strategic growth initiatives across an end-to-end P&L. It was the closest thing he had done to running a standalone public-company-style business inside a larger holding.

The actual public-company seat came next. As CEO of Primo Water Corporation, Rietbroek led the transformative merger with BlueTriton Brands that closed in November 2024 to form Primo Brands, then served as inaugural CEO of the new entity — a publicly traded leader in North American branded bottled water. Stand-up CEO work is its own discipline: integration, governance, leadership team formation, capital structure, all under quarterly scrutiny. He has documented some of those leadership lessons from brand manager to the C-suite elsewhere on the site.

Atlanta, paperboard, and the next chapter

The Graphic Packaging seat is, in some ways, the natural synthesis of everything that came before. Paper-based packaging is consumer-products-adjacent: Graphic Packaging supplies the food, beverage, household, and foodservice brands that Rietbroek spent a career building inside. It is also operationally serious — paperboard mills, converting plants, large customer relationships, real capital intensity. And it is, increasingly, a sustainability story, as the world moves away from single-use plastics toward fiber-based formats. Rietbroek has written about that transition in his perspective on the future of paper-based packaging.

Around the day job, he sits on the boards of the Metro Atlanta Chamber and the American Forest and Paper Association, is a member of the Wall Street Journal CEO Council, and holds three packaging-design patents. Periodic interviews and thought-leadership pieces appear in his Medium archive, alongside conference material on Robbert Rietbroek's SlideShare profile.

Conclusion

The boardroom version of Robbert Rietbroek's career is the one that gets summarized in a single bio paragraph for a press release. The version that actually matters is the one that explains why the bio paragraph hangs together — the through-line of brand, P&L, geography, and operating discipline that lets the same person run a baby-care business in Australia, a breakfast-foods division in North America, a bottled-water IPO, and now one of the world's largest paper-based packaging companies. None of those jobs is the same. The instinct that runs underneath them is.
